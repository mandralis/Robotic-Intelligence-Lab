Model predictive control

Model predictive control (MPC), also called receding horizon control, turns an optimal control problem into a feedback law. At each sampling instant \(t_i\) the controller

  1. 1
    measures the current state \(x(t_i)\): the cart position, both link angles, and their rates;
  2. 2
    computes the input trajectory that minimises a cost over a finite horizon \([t_i,\, t_i+T]\), using a model \(\dot x = f(x,u)\) to predict the motion and respecting the input limits;
  3. 3
    applies that input only over the first interval \([t_i,\, t_i+\Delta T]\), then repeats from the new measured state.

Each plan on its own is open loop. Feedback comes from re-solving the problem from the measured state. That is why the controller recovers from pushes it was never told about, and tolerates errors in its own model. Because the model and the constraints are used directly, the same controller swings the pendulum up from hanging and balances it at the top. In the demo \(T = 1.5\) s and \(\Delta T = 25\) ms. The faded pendulums show the current optimal trajectory \(x^*(\tau)\), \(\tau \in [t_i, t_i+T]\); toggle Show the plan to hide them.

The mathematics

Model

Let \(q = (p, \theta_1, \theta_2)\) be the cart position and the absolute link angles measured from the upright vertical, and \(x = (q, \dot q) \in \mathbb{R}^6\). The cart has mass \(m_c = 10\) kg, and point masses \(m_1 = m_2 = 1\) kg sit at the ends of massless links of length \(l_1 = l_2 = 0.5\) m. The Euler–Lagrange equations give

\[ M(q)\,\ddot q = h(q,\dot q) + e_1 u, \qquad M(q) = \begin{bmatrix} m_c + m_1 + m_2 & (m_1+m_2) l_1 \cos\theta_1 & m_2 l_2 \cos\theta_2 \\ (m_1+m_2) l_1 \cos\theta_1 & (m_1+m_2) l_1^2 & m_2 l_1 l_2 \cos(\theta_1-\theta_2) \\ m_2 l_2 \cos\theta_2 & m_2 l_1 l_2 \cos(\theta_1-\theta_2) & m_2 l_2^2 \end{bmatrix}, \] \[ h(q,\dot q) = \begin{bmatrix} (m_1+m_2) l_1 \dot\theta_1^2 \sin\theta_1 + m_2 l_2 \dot\theta_2^2 \sin\theta_2 - c_1 \dot p \\ -m_2 l_1 l_2 \dot\theta_2^2 \sin(\theta_1-\theta_2) + (m_1+m_2) g\, l_1 \sin\theta_1 - c_2 \dot\theta_1 \\ \phantom{-}m_2 l_1 l_2 \dot\theta_1^2 \sin(\theta_1-\theta_2) + m_2 g\, l_2 \sin\theta_2 - c_3 \dot\theta_2 \end{bmatrix}, \]

where \(u\) is the horizontal force on the cart, \(c_j\) are small viscous friction coefficients and \(e_1 = (1,0,0)\). Since \(M(q) \succ 0\), this gives the state-space model \(\dot x = f(x,u) = \big(\dot q,\; M(q)^{-1}(h(q,\dot q) + e_1 u)\big)\) with \(u \in \mathcal{U} = [-200, 200]\) N. Both equilibria, upright \(x = 0\) and hanging \(\theta_1 = \theta_2 = \pi\), have \(u = 0\). A push on the pendulum enters as the generalised force \(J(q)^{\mathsf T} F\), where \(J\) is the Jacobian of the pushed mass’s position.

The receding horizon problem

At each \(t_i = i\,\Delta T\) the controller solves

\[ \begin{aligned} u_{[t_i,\, t_i+\Delta T]} = \arg\min_{(x,u)} \;& \int_{t_i}^{t_i+T} L(x,u)\, d\tau \;+\; V\big(x(t_i+T)\big) \\ \text{subject to}\;\; & x(t_i) = \text{current state}, \\ & \dot x = f(x,u), \qquad u \in \mathcal U, \\ & g(x) = |p| - p_{\max} \le 0, \end{aligned} \]

with trajectory and terminal costs

\[ L(x,u) = p^2 + q_\theta \sum_{j=1}^{2} \big(1-\cos\theta_j\big) + q_v \,\lVert \dot q \rVert^2 + Q_u u^2, \qquad V(x) = \gamma \, L(x, 0), \]

with \(q_\theta = 12\), \(q_v = 0.05\), \(Q_u = 2\cdot 10^{-4}\), \(\gamma = 10\), \(T = 1.5\) s and \(p_{\max} = 1.5\) m. Near the upright equilibrium, \(L(x,u) \approx x^{\mathsf T} Q_x x + u^{\mathsf T} Q_u u\) with \(Q_x = \operatorname{diag}(1, 6, 6, 0.05, 0.05, 0.05)\), the familiar LQR weights. Away from it the cosine terms make \(L\) periodic in each angle, so every upright configuration \(\theta_j \in 2\pi\mathbb Z\) is a minimiser. After a push that sends a link over the top, the controller catches it at the next upright position instead of unwinding a full turn. The track constraint is softened with a slack \(s \ge 0\), \(|p| \le p_{\max} + s\), penalised by \(\rho s^2\) with \(\rho = 4000\), so the problem stays feasible after a large disturbance.

From optimal control to a nonlinear program

The problem above is infinite dimensional. We transcribe it with direct multiple shooting on the grid \(t_i = \tau_0 < \tau_1 < \dots < \tau_N = t_i + T\), \(\tau_{k+1} - \tau_k = h\), with \(N = 60\) and \(h = \Delta T = 25\) ms. The input is piecewise constant, \(u(\tau) = u_k\) on \([\tau_k, \tau_{k+1})\). Over each interval the dynamics are integrated by one step of the classical Runge–Kutta method,

\[ \Phi_h(x,u) = x + \tfrac{h}{6}\,(k_1 + 2k_2 + 2k_3 + k_4), \quad k_1 = f(x,u),\; k_2 = f(x + \tfrac h2 k_1, u),\; k_3 = f(x + \tfrac h2 k_2, u),\; k_4 = f(x + h k_3, u). \]

The states at the grid points are kept as decision variables, \(\xi = (x_0, u_0, x_1, u_1, \dots, u_{N-1}, x_N, s_0, \dots, s_N) \in \mathbb{R}^M\) with \(M = 7N + 6 + (N+1) = 487\), and we solve the nonlinear program

\[ \begin{aligned} \min_{\xi \in \mathbb{R}^M} \;\; & F(\xi) = \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} h\,\big(L(x_k, u_k) + \rho s_k^2\big) + V(x_N) + \rho s_N^2 \\ \text{subject to}\;\; & x_0 - x(t_i) = 0, \\ & x_{k+1} - \Phi_h(x_k, u_k) = 0, \qquad k = 0, \dots, N-1, \\ & lb \le c(\xi) \le ub: \quad |u_k| \le 200, \;\; |p_k| \le p_{\max} + s_k, \;\; s_k \ge 0 . \end{aligned} \]

The equality constraints are the “gap-closing” conditions: they force the end of each integrated interval to meet the start of the next. This sits between the two methods described in Murray’s chapter on receding horizon control. Single shooting optimises over \(u_0,\dots,u_{N-1}\) alone and integrates across the whole horizon, so for an unstable system a small change in an early input is amplified exponentially by the time it reaches the end, and the problem is badly conditioned. Direct collocation keeps the states as variables too, but represents them by polynomials and imposes \(\dot{\tilde x} = f(\tilde x, \tilde u)\) at collocation points. Multiple shooting keeps the states as variables, which gives the good conditioning of collocation, and uses an accurate integrator inside each interval. In both cases the Jacobian of the constraints is block banded, and sparse solvers exploit this.

Solving it

In Python (the companion repository) the NLP is written in CasADi, which supplies exact first and second derivatives by algorithmic differentiation, and solved with IPOPT, a primal–dual interior-point method that takes Newton steps on the perturbed KKT conditions. Between sampling instants the previous solution, shifted by one interval, \(\xi^{\text{init}} = (x_1, u_1, \dots, x_N, u_{N-1}, x_N)\), is used as the initial guess.

In the browser the same problem is solved with control-limited iterative LQR, a Gauss–Newton variant of differential dynamic programming that eliminates the states through the dynamics and solves a sequence of time-varying LQR problems. Given a nominal trajectory \((\bar x_k, \bar u_k)\), linearise \(\delta x_{k+1} = A_k\,\delta x_k + B_k\,\delta u_k\), with \(A_k = \partial \Phi_h/\partial x\) and \(B_k = \partial\Phi_h/\partial u\), and run the backward Riccati-type recursion from \(V_x = \nabla V(\bar x_N)\), \(V_{xx} = \nabla^2 V(\bar x_N)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} Q_x &= \ell_x + A_k^{\mathsf T} V_x, & Q_u &= \ell_u + B_k^{\mathsf T} V_x, \\ Q_{xx} &= \ell_{xx} + A_k^{\mathsf T} V_{xx} A_k, & Q_{uu} &= \ell_{uu} + B_k^{\mathsf T} V_{xx} B_k + \mu, \qquad Q_{ux} = B_k^{\mathsf T} V_{xx} A_k, \\ \kappa_k &= \operatorname{clip}_{\mathcal U}\!\big(\bar u_k - Q_{uu}^{-1} Q_u\big) - \bar u_k, & K_k &= \begin{cases} -Q_{uu}^{-1} Q_{ux} & \text{if the input is not saturated,} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise,} \end{cases} \\[4pt] V_x &\leftarrow Q_x + K_k^{\mathsf T} Q_{uu} \kappa_k + K_k^{\mathsf T} Q_u + Q_{ux}^{\mathsf T} \kappa_k, & V_{xx} &\leftarrow Q_{xx} + K_k^{\mathsf T} Q_{uu} K_k + K_k^{\mathsf T} Q_{ux} + Q_{ux}^{\mathsf T} K_k, \end{aligned} \]

for \(k = N-1, \dots, 0\). Here \(\ell = h L\) is the stage cost with Gauss–Newton Hessians (always positive semidefinite) and \(\mu > 0\) is a Levenberg–Marquardt regularisation. The forward pass simulates the nonlinear model under the affine feedback policy \(u_k = \bar u_k + \alpha \kappa_k + K_k (x_k - \bar x_k)\), with a backtracking line search on \(\alpha\). For linear dynamics, a quadratic cost and no input limits, the \(V_{xx}\) recursion reduces to the discrete-time Riccati equation of LQR, and one iteration is exact. In real-time fashion only up to four iterations are run per \(\Delta T\), warm-started from the shifted previous plan and its gains \(K_k\). Each step takes about a millisecond.

Stability and the terminal cost

A finite horizon can make the optimal short-term behaviour lead away from the long-term goal. Theorem 4.1 of Murray’s notes gives a sufficient condition: if the terminal cost is a control Lyapunov function satisfying

\[ \min_{u \in \mathcal U} \Big(\frac{\partial V}{\partial x} f(x,u) + L(x,u)\Big) \le 0 \]

in a neighbourhood of the origin, the receding horizon trajectories converge to zero exponentially for every \(T > 0\) and \(\Delta T \in (0, T]\). A natural choice is \(V(x) = x^{\mathsf T} P x\), with \(P\) the solution of the algebraic Riccati equation for the linearisation at the upright equilibrium. The demo uses the simpler, heuristic \(V = \gamma L(x,0)\) together with a horizon long enough to make the swing-up feasible. Replacing it with the Riccati terminal cost is a good exercise.

Further reading

Richard Murray’s Optimization-Based Control, the supplement to Åström and Murray’s Feedback Systems and the notes for Caltech’s CDS 112, is an excellent resource for learning more on this topic. It covers trajectory generation, optimal control, receding horizon control (including the stability theory above) and Kalman filtering. The notation on this page follows it.
